How a Roof Claim Works in Ohio

A roof claim is a request for your insurer to pay for sudden, accidental damage — usually wind, hail or a falling tree. It is not a way to replace a roof that has simply worn out. Because most homeowners go years between claims, the process can feel unfamiliar. Here is how it typically runs:

  • Storm hits. You document damage and protect the home from further water entry.
  • A contractor inspects and photographs the roof so you know whether there is storm damage worth claiming.
  • You report the claim to your insurer, by phone, app or agent.
  • The insurer acknowledges the claim and assigns an adjuster, who inspects the roof.
  • The insurer issues an estimate and a decision; payment follows, often in two parts on replacement-cost policies.
  • Repairs are completed, and any withheld depreciation is released once you submit the final invoice.

The Deadlines Ohio Insurers Must Meet

Ohio Administrative Code 3901-1-54 sets the pace for property claims. It is worth knowing these dates, because they let you follow up with confidence instead of waiting indefinitely.

The clock for a decision starts when the insurer receives proof of loss, so keep a record of what you sent and when. If a deadline passes without word, a polite written follow-up that cites the rule usually gets things moving. If you believe a claim is being handled unfairly, the Ohio Department of Insurance takes consumer complaints.

Claims After Delaware County Storms

The March 14, 2024 EF1 tornado is the clearest recent example of why documentation matters here. The National Weather Service traced it from near US-23 south of Delaware through Berlin Township and past Sunbury and Galena, with peak winds of 110 mph. The worst damage was obvious, but homes along the edges of a path like that often end up with loosened shingles and lifted flashing that nobody photographed at the time.

Good claims are built in the first days after the storm. Photograph the roof, gutters, siding and any debris before cleanup, keep receipts for emergency roof tarping, and write down the date and time of the storm. Our after-the-storm checklist walks through each step. If you call (740) 555-0177 after a storm, we inspect, photograph every slope and give you a written report you can submit with your claim.

Deductibles

Wind and Hail Deductibles Can Be Larger Than You Expect

Many homeowners policies carry a separate wind and hail deductible. The Ohio Department of Insurance explains that it may be a flat dollar amount or a percentage of your dwelling's insured value. A 2% deductible on a $300,000 dwelling limit is $6,000 — and with home values in communities like Genoa Township, where the township reports a median home value of $437,800, percentage deductibles climb quickly. Read your declarations page before a storm, not after.

Your deductible is your share of the loss, and your policy expects you to pay it. We bill the real cost of the work and do not offer to "cover," rebate or absorb deductibles. If a contractor offers that, treat it as a warning sign about how the rest of the claim will be handled.

Getting paid

Replacement Cost, Depreciation and the Repair Deadline

On a replacement-cost policy, insurers often pay the actual cash value first — the replacement price minus depreciation for the roof's age — and release the withheld depreciation after the work is done. The Ohio Department of Insurance notes that these policies often require repairs within a set time, such as 180 days, to recover the full amount. If your roof is an aging original from the 1998–2008 building boom, depreciation can be a large share of the claim, so missing that deadline can be costly. Our builder-grade roof lifespan guide explains how age plays into the decision.

If your mortgage company is named on the check, expect it to require an endorsement and sometimes an inspection before releasing funds. Ask your lender about its process early so the money does not stall.

Adjuster visit

Preparing for the Adjuster's Inspection

The Ohio Department of Insurance advises homeowners to be present for inspections. Have your storm photos ready, and ask your roofer to attend if possible so the adjuster sees the same damage and measurements. A thorough adjuster's estimate should account for more than shingles: tear-off, underlayment, the ice barrier the Residential Code of Ohio requires at the eaves, drip edge, flashing, vents, pipe boots, and the permit. Every Delaware County re-roof requires building-department approval, so the permit is a legitimate cost — see our roof permit guide.

When an estimate leaves out code-required items or measures the roof short, a contractor can submit a supplement with photos and measurements. That is a normal part of the process, not a dispute.

On the job

When homeowners call us after the adjuster has already come and gone, the most common gap we find is not the shingle count — it is the items that only show up once you think about how a two-story Delaware County roof actually gets rebuilt: steep-pitch charges, starter strip along the rakes, ice barrier at the eaves, the extra valley metal. None of that is padding; it is the work. We put each item next to a photo so the adjuster can see why it is there.

Getting help

Public Adjusters and What Contractors Can and Cannot Do

A public adjuster is a professional you hire to represent you in the claim, usually for a percentage of the settlement. In Ohio, no one may act as a public insurance adjuster without a certificate of authority from the Superintendent of Insurance under Ohio Revised Code 3951.02. If someone offers to "handle" or "negotiate" your claim, ask whether they are a licensed public adjuster and verify it with the Ohio Department of Insurance. Public adjusters can make sense on large or disputed losses, such as a home with roof, siding and interior damage; on a straightforward roof claim, many homeowners do fine on their own with good documentation.

A roofing contractor's role is narrower: inspect, document, estimate the work, and explain the scope to the adjuster. The Ohio Department of Insurance advises homeowners not to give contractors power of attorney over a claim. We provide storm damage documentation and meet the adjuster on site, but the claim remains yours.

Contracts

Your Three-Day Right to Cancel

Many storm contracts are signed at the kitchen table. Under Ohio's Home Solicitation Sales Act (R.C. 1345.23), when a sale is made at your home you may generally cancel at any time before midnight of the third business day after signing, and the contract must include a notice of that right. Read any "contingency agreement" carefully before you sign it, and never sign one before you have seen an inspection report.

Frequently asked

Is it illegal in Ohio for a roofer to pay my deductible?

Ohio has no enacted statute written specifically about roofers and deductibles — bills have been proposed but not passed. That does not make it a good idea: your policy expects you to pay your deductible, and misrepresenting the cost of a job to an insurer creates real risk. We charge the real price.

Will filing a roof claim raise my premium?

It depends on your insurer and your claim history, and we can't predict it. Ask your agent before you file. That is another reason to get a free roof inspection first — so you only claim real storm damage.

What if the insurer denies my roof claim?

Ask for the denial in writing with the policy language it relies on. If you believe damage was missed, you can request a re-inspection with your contractor present, submit additional photos, or consult a licensed public adjuster.

Do I have to use the roofer my insurance company recommends?

No. You choose your contractor. Get a written scope, proof of insurance and a clear warranty, and make sure the contractor will pull the right Delaware County permit.

Can you meet my adjuster at the house?

Yes. We routinely meet adjusters on site with our photos and measurements. Call (740) 555-0177 to schedule the inspection and let us know the adjuster's appointment time.
