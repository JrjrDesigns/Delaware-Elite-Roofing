Stop the water

When your roof is open, the clock is running

A roof that has lost a section of shingles, had decking punctured by a limb, or been partially peeled back by wind will let water into the attic with the next rain – and in central Ohio storm season, the next rain is rarely far behind. Wet insulation, soaked drywall and ruined ceilings can quickly cost more than the roof damage itself.

Your homeowners policy almost certainly expects you to take reasonable steps to prevent further damage after a loss. A properly installed emergency tarp is the standard way to do that. It is not a repair; it is a bridge that keeps the house dry while you document the damage, file your claim and schedule the permanent work.

Why it matters here

Delaware County storms that opened roofs

The March 14, 2024 EF1 tornado showed what a single night can do. The National Weather Service documented partial roof removal and blown-out windows in neighborhoods along its roughly 36-mile path from south of Delaware through Berlin Township, past Sunbury and Galena, with peak winds of 110 mph. At least 254 homes and businesses were affected. Every one of those open roofs needed to be dried in before the next rain.

The smaller tornadoes did the same kind of damage on a smaller scale. The September 7, 2020 EF0 east of Delaware partially lifted a home's roof and scattered shingles. The August 20, 2016 EF0 near Delaware Lake and the Delaware Dam caused roof damage from fallen trees. And central Ohio's big regional windstorms – the June 2012 derecho with gusts of 70–80+ mph and the 2008 remnants of Hurricane Ike – remind everyone that a calm summer evening can turn quickly.

Exposure varies across the county, and so does what we bring to the job. Homes near Hoover Reservoir in Genoa Township and near O'Shaughnessy Reservoir in Concord Township face wind coming off open water. Two-story subdivision homes from the 1998–2008 build-out often have steep, tall roofs that need roof anchors and longer ladders just to reach the ridge. Older homes in the village cores may have brittle, layered roofing that tears easily if a tarp is fastened carelessly. We size the crew and the materials to the house before we leave the shop.

On the job

When homeowners in Delaware's older tree-lined neighborhoods call us after a storm, it is usually a limb, not the wind itself, that opened the roof. The big mature trees in the older parts of the city and around Delaware Lake are beautiful, but a heavy branch dropped onto a roof punches through shingles and decking in one shot. Those holes need the debris cleared carefully, the broken decking cut back so nothing sharp tears the tarp, and the tarp run well past the damage – a small patch over a ragged hole rarely survives the next gust.

How we do it

A tarp that actually holds

Most failed tarps fail for the same reasons: they were nailed directly through the plastic, they did not run over the ridge, or they left an edge where wind could get underneath. A tarp that comes loose in the night is worse than no tarp at all, because it can tear off more shingles on the way out. Here is how we install one.

  • Photograph the damage first, before anything is covered, so your adjuster can see it
  • Remove loose debris and broken shingle pieces that would puncture the tarp
  • Use heavy-duty, UV-resistant tarps sized well beyond the damaged area
  • Run the tarp up and over the ridge whenever possible so water sheds over it, not under it
  • Fasten through wood battens rather than through bare plastic, so wind load spreads across the board
  • Seal and weight the leading edges on the windward side
  • Check the attic and interior for active water and point out anything that needs a mitigation company
On the job

In our experience with newer homes on open ground around Northstar and the other subdivisions outside Sunbury, wind is the enemy of a tarp long after the storm has passed. With wide lots and almost no mature trees, a steady breeze comes across the fields and works at any edge it can find. On those roofs we use more battens than we would on a sheltered street, keep the windward edge tucked and secured, and come back to check the tarp after any significant wind until the permanent repair is done.

Next steps

From tarp to permanent repair

Once the house is dry, the photos and notes from the tarping visit become the start of your insurance documentation. Our storm damage roof repair service picks up from there: a full roof inspection, a written scope for your adjuster, and a meeting on the roof if you want one. The roof insurance claims guide explains Ohio's claim timelines – insurers must acknowledge a claim within 15 days under OAC 3901-1-54 – and our storm damage checklist covers what to do in the first 48 hours.

A tarp is temporary. Heavy-duty tarps hold for weeks, sometimes a few months, but sun and wind wear them down, and replacement-cost policies often expect repairs to be finished within a set period, such as 180 days. When the claim is settled we handle the permanent work, whether that is a targeted roof repair or a full roof replacement, along with the permit from the correct office.

Safety

What to do while you wait for us

Please do not climb onto a wet or storm-damaged roof yourself. Wind-damaged decking can be weaker than it looks, and wet shingles are slick. From inside, move furniture and electronics away from active leaks, put buckets under drips, and if a ceiling is sagging with water, keep people out of that room. If water is near light fixtures or outlets, shut off power to that area at the panel. Take photos and video of interior damage – they matter for the claim as much as the roof photos do.

Frequently asked

Do you really answer storm calls at night?

Yes. We take storm calls 24/7. Night tarping depends on conditions – we will not put a crew on a roof during lightning or in dangerous wind – but we will triage your call and get out at first safe opportunity. Call (740) 555-0177.

Will my insurance pay for emergency tarping?

Many policies cover reasonable temporary repairs to prevent further damage after a covered loss, but coverage varies. Keep the invoice and photos and submit them with your claim. Our insurance claims guide explains the process.

How long can a tarp stay on my roof?

A properly battened heavy-duty tarp can last weeks or a few months, but it is temporary. Sun and wind degrade it, and your policy may set a deadline for completing repairs, so plan the permanent fix promptly.

A tree limb fell on my roof. Should I remove it first?

Large limbs should be removed by a tree service or by us with proper equipment – pulling a limb out can let more water in or collapse damaged framing. Photograph it in place before anything moves.

Can I tarp the roof myself?

We do not recommend it. Storm-damaged roofs are unsafe to walk, and a tarp nailed through the plastic usually tears loose in the next wind. If you must cover something from a ladder, keep it small and call a pro as soon as possible.

Do you tarp roofs in rural parts of the county?

Yes. We cover all of Delaware County, including the villages and open township land in the north and west. Outbuilding roofs can be tarped too, though we prioritize occupied homes during a widespread storm.
