Finding the source

Why roof leaks are hard to pin down

Water is patient. It slips under a lifted shingle or a gap in the flashing, runs down the underlayment or along a rafter, and finally drips onto the drywall several feet away from where it entered. That is why so many leak repairs fail: someone smears sealant on the shingles directly above the stain, the stain comes back with the next storm, and the homeowner concludes the whole roof is shot.

Our approach is methodical. We start in the attic when it is accessible, looking for stained sheathing, rusted nail tips and wet insulation, and follow the water path uphill. Then we go on the roof and inspect every penetration, wall intersection and valley above that path. When the source is not obvious, we water-test sections of the roof in sequence until the leak shows itself. You get photos of what we found before we fix anything.

If you have water coming through a ceiling right now, call (740) 555-0177. If the opening is large or the weather is not cooperating, we can secure it first with emergency roof tarping and come back to make the permanent repair.

Usual suspects

Where Delaware County roofs actually leak

Most of the homes we work on in Genoa Township, Orange, Liberty and Concord townships were built between the mid-1990s and late 2000s, and they leak in predictable places. Older homes in Delaware, Sunbury and the villages leak in different ones.

Newer homes are not immune. Big two-story houses in Powell and Liberty Township often carry complex rooflines – multiple gables, dormers, a bonus room over the garage – and every one of those intersections is another place for water to get in. Add a decade or two of wind across open lots, and a leak at a dormer wall or a valley is far more likely than a leak in the middle of a plain slope. That is where we look first.

  • Step flashing where a lower roof, porch or garage meets a two-story wall – often missing a kick-out flashing at the bottom
  • Plumbing vent boots whose rubber collars have cracked with age
  • Valleys where builder-grade shingles have worn thin under concentrated water flow
  • Chimney flashing and crickets on older homes, and on fireplaces with framed chases
  • Skylight curbs and their original flashing kits
  • Box vents, ridge vents and bath fan exhaust caps
  • Eaves where ice dams back water under the shingles in winter
  • Nail holes from satellite dishes, holiday lights and old antenna mounts
On the job

When homeowners in Lewis Center ask us why the wall under a second-floor window is wet when the ceiling is dry, the answer is usually the spot where a garage or porch roof dies into the side of the house. Many two-story homes from the Orange Township building boom were finished without a kick-out flashing at the bottom of that step flashing, so roof runoff gets dumped behind the siding instead of into the gutter. The water rots the sheathing inside the wall for years before anyone sees a stain, which is why we pull a few courses of siding back when the signs point there.

Winter

Ice dams and winter leaks

Central Ohio winters hover around freezing for weeks at a time, and Columbus-area normals put annual snowfall at about 28 inches. When heat escaping from a poorly insulated or poorly ventilated attic melts snow on the upper roof, that meltwater refreezes at the cold eave and builds an ice dam. Water pools behind the dam and works its way up under the shingles.

The Residential Code of Ohio addresses this directly: re-roofs require an ice barrier running from the eave edge to at least 24 inches inside the exterior wall line. Many older roofs – and some first-generation subdivision roofs – do not have one, or have one that stops short over a deep overhang. A winter leak at the eave is a strong hint that the ice barrier is missing or inadequate.

For a single leak we can usually repair the eave area. If the roof is near the end of its life anyway, a full roof replacement is the time to install the code-required ice barrier along every eave and in every valley – and if your gutters overflow and ice up, our gutter service addresses that side of the problem.

Older homes

Leaks in Delaware's historic neighborhoods and village cores

The City of Delaware has more than 500 homes and carriage houses listed on the National Register in its Historic Northwest Neighborhood, and the old town cores of Sunbury, Galena, Ashley and Ostrander date to the early and mid-1800s. Leaks on these houses tend to come from chimneys, old flashing details, roof-to-roof junctions where additions were built later, and decades of layered repairs.

Any roof work on a property in Delaware's Downtown Historic District needs a Certificate of Appropriateness from the city before work begins. Roof projects are often approved administratively, but the application still has to happen first, and we help prepare it.

On the job

In our experience with Delaware's Northwest Neighborhood , the roof that gets forgotten is the one on the carriage house out back. It does not have a finished ceiling, nobody goes up into it, and a leak there can run for years before the owner notices a soft spot in the floor or a rotted sill plate. When we look at the main house roof in that part of town, we ask to look at the outbuilding too – it is often in worse shape.

After the fix

Repair, document, and decide what's next

Once the source is confirmed, we make the repair with proper materials – new flashing, new boots, new shingles woven or cut into the valley correctly – rather than a tube of sealant. If tracing the leak reveals that the roof is failing in many places at once, we will say so. Frequently asked

My ceiling stain only shows up during heavy wind-driven rain. What does that mean?

Leaks that appear only in wind-driven rain usually point to flashing at walls, chimneys or dormers, or to shingles whose seal strips have let go so wind can push water under them. Those are common on exposed Delaware County lots. We can water-test the suspect areas to confirm.

Is a roof leak covered by homeowners insurance?

Sudden damage from wind or hail often is; slow leaks from wear and age usually are not. Policies vary, so check yours. Our roof insurance claims guide explains how the Ohio claim process and timelines work.

How fast can you get out for an active leak?

Active leaks get priority scheduling. Call (740) 555-0177, tell us water is coming in, and we will arrange the earliest visit we can, with a temporary tarp if the permanent repair has to wait for dry weather.

Why does my roof leak only in winter?

Winter-only leaks near the eaves are classic ice-dam behavior: meltwater backs up behind ice at the gutter line and seeps under the shingles. The fix usually combines eave repair, attic ventilation and insulation improvements, and an ice barrier at the next re-roof.

Can a small leak really cause serious damage?

Yes. Slow leaks soak insulation, rot sheathing and framing, and can lead to mold well before a ceiling stain appears. Wall leaks at missing kick-out flashing are especially sneaky because the water stays hidden behind siding.

Will you just caulk the leak?

No. Sealant has its place on a few details, but a proper repair replaces failed flashing, boots or shingles. A caulk-only fix tends to fail within a season or two and usually hides the real problem.
