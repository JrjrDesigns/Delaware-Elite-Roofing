Why metal

Why Delaware County homeowners are looking at metal

Delaware County is not a place where most people grew up thinking about metal roofs on houses. The southern townships filled with asphalt-shingled subdivision homes during the 1990s and 2000s, and the older homes in Delaware, Sunbury and the villages were roofed with whatever was standard in their day. But three things are pushing more homeowners to ask about metal now.

The first is wind. The March 14, 2024 EF1 tornado reached 110 mph and tracked about 36 miles from south of Delaware through Berlin Township and near Sunbury and Galena; EF0s hit near Delaware Lake in 2016 and east of the city in 2020. Homes on open former farmland with young trees take those winds head-on, and a properly fastened standing-seam roof has no tabs or seal strips to let go. The second is timing: a huge share of the county's builder-grade shingle roofs from the 1998–2008 build-out are being replaced at once, and some owners would rather make this the last roof they buy. The third is style – the modern farmhouse look that shows up in newer Berlin Township subdivisions pairs naturally with metal accents and full metal roofs.

Metal is not right for every house or every budget, and we will tell you when it is not. Call (740) 555-0177 for a free estimate that compares metal and architectural shingles side by side for your roof.

Options

Metal roofing systems we install

Not all metal roofs are the same, and the right choice depends on whether you are roofing a house, a porch, a garage or a barn.

On a house, a metal roof is still a re-roof under the Residential Code of Ohio. That means the old shingles come off rather than staying underneath, the decking gets inspected and repaired, and the code-required ice barrier goes in from the eave edge to at least 24 inches inside the exterior wall line, followed by a high-temperature underlayment rated for metal. Skipping those steps to save money defeats the point of buying a roof meant to last decades. Two-story subdivision homes in Lewis Center and Orange Township also tend to have several valleys and dormers, which call for careful custom flashing.

  • Standing-seam (concealed fastener) – the premium residential choice; fasteners sit under the raised seams, so nothing is exposed to weather
  • Snap-lock and mechanically seamed profiles for low-slope porches and additions
  • Exposed-fastener ribbed panels for barns, pole buildings, sheds and detached garages
  • Metal accent roofs over porches, bay windows and entries on shingled homes
  • Matching trim, ridge, eave and gable flashing, plus snow guards where sliding snow could be a hazard
  • Repair, re-fastening and re-screwing of older exposed-fastener roofs

The open townships

Barns, pole buildings and outbuildings

Outside the subdivisions, much of Delaware County is still farm and large-lot country – most unincorporated land was originally zoned for one- to five-acre lots, and about 71 percent of Liberty Township's acreage is zoned for one-acre farm residence. That means a lot of barns, pole buildings, horse sheds and big detached garages. When the 2024 tornado came through, the National Weather Service documented barns destroyed along its path, and the 2016 EF0 near Delaware Lake caused a partial barn roof collapse. Outbuildings take the brunt of open-country wind.

We re-roof and repair outbuildings across the north and west of the county around Ashley and Ostrander, and in the open parts of Berlin Township and Liberty Township. Detached accessory structures without conditioned space are generally exempt from the ice-barrier requirement that applies to houses, but they still need sound purlins, correct fastener spacing and properly lapped panels to survive a spring storm.

On the job

In our experience with pole barns and outbuildings in the rural townships , the panels are rarely what fails first – it is the screws. The rubber washers on exposed fasteners dry out and crack long before the steel wears out, and every one becomes a small leak. Wind then works the loosened screws until the holes wallow out. Re-screwing an outbuilding with new fasteners, upsized where the holes have enlarged, can add years to a panel roof that otherwise looks ready to be torn off.

Permits and approvals

Switching to metal: the review you need to know about

Changing roof material is where paperwork gets real. In Powell, every re-roof requires a permit, and the city's re-roof handout states that roofing material must be the same as what was previously installed unless structural modifications are made. Going from shingles to metal there means a conversation with the building department before you sign anything. The City of Delaware, Westerville and Delaware County Building Safety each have their own process, and Delaware's Downtown Historic District requires a Certificate of Appropriateness for any roof change before work begins.

Homeowners associations matter just as much. Many Delaware County communities review exterior material and color changes, and in Scioto Reserve any change in shingle color must be approved by the Design Review Board. A switch to metal is a bigger change than a new shingle color, so we prepare a full submittal with product data and color samples. Our HOA roof replacement page explains that process, and the Delaware County roof permit guide covers which office handles your address.

On the job

When homeowners in Powell ask us about replacing their shingles with standing seam, the first thing we do is slow them down. Between the city's same-material language and a neighborhood design review board, a metal roof in Powell can take more approvals than the installation takes days. We would rather spend a few weeks getting the city and the board on board than have a homeowner put down a deposit on a roof they are not allowed to install.

Cost

What metal roofing costs

Metal costs more up front than asphalt. Central Ohio estimates range widely – published figures run from roughly $7,200 to just under $20,000 for typical residential metal roofs, and local contractors quote standing-seam homes in the $18,000 to $30,000-plus range. Profile, gauge, roof size, pitch and the number of penetrations all move the number. Exposed-fastener panels on outbuildings cost considerably less per square foot than standing seam.

Compare that to the $8,000 to $18,000 most central Ohio asphalt re-roofs fall into, and the decision comes down to how long you plan to stay and how much the wind exposure worries you. Frequently asked

Is a metal roof loud in the rain?

Over solid decking and underlayment, as with residential standing seam, a metal roof is usually not much louder than shingles from inside the house. Open-framed barns with panels fastened directly to purlins are louder, which is normal for outbuildings.

Can I put a metal roof on my Powell home?

Possibly, but Powell's re-roof rules say roofing material must match what was previously installed unless structural modifications are made, so a material change needs review with the city first – and possibly your HOA. We help you sort that out before you commit. Call (740) 555-0177.

Does metal hold up better in high wind?

A correctly installed, properly fastened standing-seam roof has no tabs or seal strips to lift, which makes it a strong choice for open, wind-exposed lots. Fastening pattern, edge details and deck attachment are what make the difference.

Do you repair old barn roofs, or only replace them?

Both. Many older panel roofs can be re-screwed, re-sealed at laps and patched with matching panels. If rust has eaten through or the purlins have failed, replacement makes more sense, and we will show you which applies.

Will hail dent a metal roof?

Large hail can cosmetically dent some metal panels, especially thinner gauges. Thicker gauges and textured finishes resist it better. Ask your insurer how your policy treats cosmetic damage on metal before you choose.

Do I need a permit for a metal roof on a detached barn?

Requirements for agricultural and accessory buildings vary, so we check with Delaware County Building Safety or the city before work starts. The ice-barrier rule that applies to houses generally does not apply to detached structures without conditioned space.
